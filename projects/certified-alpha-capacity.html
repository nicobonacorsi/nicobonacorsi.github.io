Abstract

Statistical validation takes time, and alpha can decay before the evidence justifies deployment. We quantify the surviving opportunity through Certified Alpha Capacity: the maximum expected value remaining under prescribed false-deployment and power constraints. In a canonical Gaussian experiment, we derive an exact certification frontier and the sharp law \(\mathcal K_{\alpha,\beta}/\log(1/\varepsilon)\) for residual information capacity just above it. Near the frontier, a single interim decision can preserve orders of magnitude more capacity than fixed-time testing. Mapping information into cash reveals why equally certifiable signals can retain different economic values. In the specified competitive equilibrium, lifetime information approaches the certification frontier exponentially as research costs vanish.

AlphaValue is the reference implementation: a Python package, command-line interface, public test suite, numerical theory checks, reproducibility materials, and an interactive Alpha Survival Frontier calculator.

Question

Can an alpha generate enough future information to meet a declared false-deployment and power target before the opportunity reaches its economic endpoint?

Core idea

Put statistical evidence and economic lifetime on the same information clock. In the canonical Gaussian experiment, reliable certification requires lifetime KL information to exceed an exact threshold:

\[ \mathcal I_{\mathrm{life}} \ge \mathcal I_{\mathrm{crit}}(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{1}{2} \left(z_{1-\alpha}+z_{1-\beta}\right)^2. \]

Under zero-hurdle exponential decay, lifetime information becomes a function of initial Sharpe and half-life. This produces the Alpha Survival Frontier and makes the statistical cost of search breadth explicit when many candidate signals are screened. Just above the frontier, the optimal residual information value obeys the inverse-logarithmic law \(c_{\alpha,\beta}(A_*+\varepsilon)\sim \mathcal K_{\alpha,\beta}/\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

Main results

  • Exact Gaussian certification threshold and a general KL data-processing converse.
  • Sharp near-frontier capacity: residual information value scales as \(\mathcal K_{\alpha,\beta}/\log(1/\varepsilon)\), attained by a conditional-error crossing rule.
  • Cash-value transfer laws that separate statistical certifiability from implementable economic value, including the \([\log(1/\varepsilon)]^{-2/3}\) transverse-crossing scale.
  • Robust certification results for ordered decay families and a least-favorable-mixture characterization for nonordered alternatives.
  • Competitive crowding equilibria in which residual lifetime information concentrates exponentially near the certification frontier as research costs vanish.
